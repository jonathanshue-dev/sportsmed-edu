## Key points

- Regular training makes the heart larger, stronger and slower-beating. This is called the **athlete's heart**, and it changes the ECG too.
- Many ECG findings that would be flagged as "abnormal" in a non-athlete are **normal training adaptations** in an athlete and do not mean you need to stop training.
- A small number of findings are **warning signs** that may point to hidden heart disease (such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) and need further tests.
- There is an international standard designed specifically for reading athletes' ECGs (the **2017 International Criteria**), which greatly reduces false alarms. (Evidence level: international expert consensus)
- A normal ECG is not a 100% guarantee: **chest pain, fainting or palpitations during exercise** always matter more than the ECG.

## What is it?

An electrocardiogram (ECG) records the heart's electrical signal through stickers placed on the chest, arms and legs. It takes a few minutes, does not hurt, and is a common part of an athlete's pre-participation exam.

The main goal of an athlete's ECG is to find the few heart conditions that **cause no symptoms day to day but can trigger sudden cardiac arrest during intense exercise**, for example:

- **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy**: abnormal thickening of the heart muscle
- **Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy**: heart muscle gradually replaced by fat and scar tissue
- **Channelopathies**: inherited electrical disorders such as long QT syndrome and Brugada syndrome
- **Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) pattern**: an extra electrical pathway in the heart

The problem is that **training itself changes the ECG**. Reading an athlete's ECG with standards built for the general population can label many healthy athletes as having heart disease, leading to unnecessary bans and tests. That is why athlete-specific criteria exist.

## The athlete's heart: which changes are normal?

The findings below are common in trained athletes, especially in endurance sports (distance running, cycling, rowing, cross-country skiing, swimming). **On their own, in an athlete with no symptoms and no family history, they need no further testing:**

- **Slow heart rate (sinus bradycardia)**: a resting rate in the 40s, or even the 30s, can be normal. It means the heart pumps more blood with each beat.
- **Heart rate that varies with breathing (sinus arrhythmia)**: faster when breathing in, slower when breathing out. A normal reflex.
- **High voltages**: tall ECG waves that might suggest an enlarged heart in a non-athlete, but usually just reflect a heart muscle strengthened by training.
- **Early repolarization**: a slight elevation of one part of the tracing, especially common in young, male and Black athletes.
- **Incomplete right bundle branch block**: a pattern showing slightly slower conduction on the right side of the heart.
- **First-degree AV block and Mobitz type I (Wenckebach) second-degree AV block**: the signal from the upper to the lower chambers is slower and occasionally skips a beat. Common in well-trained athletes and usually disappears with exercise.
- **T-wave inversion in leads V1–V3 in athletes under 16.**
- **In Black athletes**, ST elevation followed by T-wave inversion in leads V1–V4.

What these have in common: they reflect a **benign adaptation** to training and often partly reverse when training is reduced.

### "Borderline" findings

A few findings are classed as borderline, such as axis deviation, atrial enlargement patterns and complete right bundle branch block.

- **Just one of them**: in an athlete without symptoms or family history, usually no further testing is needed.
- **Two or more together**: further evaluation is recommended.

## Which ECG findings need further tests?

The findings below are **not** caused by training. Whether or not the athlete has symptoms, they should be evaluated by a doctor experienced in sports cardiology:

- **T-wave inversion** in the lateral or inferior leads (e.g. V5–V6, II, aVF): one of the most important warning signs and a possible early sign of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
- **ST-segment depression**
- **Pathological Q waves**
- **Complete left bundle branch block**, or markedly slowed ventricular conduction (QRS ≥ 140 ms)
- **Pre-excitation** (WPW pattern)
- **Prolonged QT interval** (corrected QT ≥ 470 ms in men, ≥ 480 ms in women)
- **Brugada type 1 pattern**
- **Extremely slow heart rate** (under 30 beats per minute), or PR interval ≥ 400 ms
- **Mobitz type II second-degree or third-degree AV block**
- **Frequent premature ventricular beats** (2 or more on a 10-second ECG)
- **Fast atrial or ventricular arrhythmias**

(Classification based on the 2017 International Criteria; international expert consensus)

## What if I'm told my ECG is abnormal?

Don't panic. An ECG is a screening test, not a diagnosis. Most athletes flagged for an abnormal ECG turn out to be normal on further testing.

**Common next steps:**

- **Echocardiogram**: an ultrasound of the heart's structure, wall thickness and valves. Usually arranged first.
- **Cardiac MRI**: for a clear warning sign such as lateral T-wave inversion, MRI is usually done **alongside** the echo, not only after it. It is more sensitive for early cardiomyopathy, so a normal echo does not rule it out. (Evidence level: international expert consensus)
- **Exercise ECG** and **24–48 hour ECG monitoring** (Holter): to look at heart rhythm during exercise and daily life.
- **Family screening**: if an inherited heart condition is suspected, first-degree relatives may need testing too.

**Long-term follow-up:**

An Italian follow-up study found that among athletes with marked repolarization changes on the ECG but no heart disease found at the time, a few developed cardiomyopathy over an average of 9 years, while none of the athletes with normal ECGs did. So even when initial tests are normal, these athletes should have **regular follow-up** rather than a one-time all-clear. (Evidence level: observational study) Experts typically suggest **a yearly echocardiogram and a cardiac MRI every 2–4 years**. (Evidence level: expert consensus)

**Can I keep playing?**

- An "abnormal ECG" does not automatically mean "no sport".
- The 2025 recommendations from the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) emphasize **shared decision-making**: the doctor and athlete weigh the risks together and decide whether and how to keep competing, rather than applying blanket bans.

## When to see a doctor (warning signs)

> **If any of these apply, stop intense exercise and see a doctor promptly:**
>
> - **Chest pain or tightness** during or after exercise
> - **Fainting** or near-fainting during exercise
> - **Sudden racing or irregular heartbeat** during exercise that doesn't settle with rest
> - Breathlessness **clearly out of proportion** to the effort
> - A family member who **died suddenly and unexpectedly before age 50**, or a known inherited heart condition
> - An ECG report that recommends further testing that you haven't completed yet

## Self-care and exercise advice

- **Be honest about your medical and family history**: a family history of sudden death is one of the most important clues to an inherited heart condition.
- **Tell the doctor you are an athlete**: mention your sport and weekly training load so the right criteria are used.
- **Have the ECG read by someone familiar with athletes' ECGs**: standard criteria often mislabel normal training changes as abnormal.
- **Keep your old ECGs**: comparing over time is more informative than a single tracing.
- **Stimulants and some medicines or supplements** can affect heart rhythm. Mention them to your doctor.
- **Have an AED on site**: no screening prevents every sudden cardiac arrest, so training and competition venues should have CPR-trained staff and an automated external defibrillator (AED).

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: My resting heart rate is 45. Is something wrong with my heart?**

For a regularly training athlete, a resting rate in the 40s is common and reflects good fitness. As long as you have no dizziness, fainting or sudden drop in performance, it is usually nothing to worry about. A rate below 30, or one that comes with symptoms, needs a medical review.

**Q: Does everyone need an ECG before starting exercise?**

Not necessarily. For the general public, a simple screening questionnaire is usually enough — see our article on pre-participation health screening. ECGs are mainly used in pre-participation exams for competitive athletes, or when a doctor thinks one is needed.

**Q: Should every athlete have an ECG?**

Countries differ. Europe (e.g. Italy) has long included the ECG in standard athlete screening, and after Italy introduced it, sudden cardiac death among young athletes fell markedly (observational study). The 2025 US recommendations consider adding an ECG "reasonable", provided trained readers and access to follow-up testing are available.

**Q: If my ECG is normal, is my heart definitely fine?**

No. An ECG cannot detect every cause of sudden death, such as an abnormal origin of a coronary artery. Even with a normal ECG, chest pain or fainting during exercise needs immediate medical attention.
